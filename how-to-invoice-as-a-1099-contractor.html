How to invoice as a 1099 contractor: a guide for independent clinicians

Updated November 19, 2026 · General information, not tax or legal advice

When you move from W-2 to 1099 work, nobody runs payroll for you anymore. You get paid when you send an invoice and the client pays it. Here's how that works, written for nurse practitioners, PAs, CRNAs, physicians and other independent clinicians, though it applies to any 1099 contractor.

Invoice, W-9 and 1099-NEC: three different documents

There's no special "1099 invoice." The "1099" describes how your client reports what it paid you, not the invoice itself.

InvoiceForm W-9Form 1099-NEC
Who fills it outYouYouYour client (the payer)
Who gets itYour clientYour clientThe IRS, with a copy to you
WhenEach shift, week or assignmentOnce, when the client asks, usually during onboardingAfter the year ends
What it showsWork done, amount, due date, how to payYour name, tax classification and taxpayer IDWhat the client paid you that year

For payments made after December 31, 2025, a business generally files Form 1099-NEC when it pays a contractor at least $2,000 in a year for services (the threshold was $600 before). All of your income is reportable whether or not you receive a 1099. Source: IRS Instructions for Forms 1099-MISC and 1099-NEC.

How to invoice, step by step

  1. Get the terms in writing first. Rate, premium or overtime rules, call pay, guaranteed hours, rounding, payment terms and how often to invoice.
  2. Collect the billing details during onboarding. The legal name of the facility or group, the accounts payable contact and email, and any PO or vendor number.
  3. Send a W-9 when asked. Use the same name on your W-9 and your invoices so payments match.
  4. Track every shift as it happens. Start, end, breaks and call. See our time tracking guide.
  5. Build the invoice from those records at the end of each billing period.
  6. Send it to the billing contact, not just the department that scheduled you, and note the date you sent it.
  7. Record payments when they arrive, including partial payments, and follow up on anything past due.

What goes on the invoice

  • The word "Invoice," a unique invoice number, the invoice date and the due date
  • Your legal or business name (matching your W-9), address, email and phone
  • The client's legal name, billing contact and billing address or AP email
  • PO, contract or vendor number, if the client uses one
  • The service period and one line per shift or service: date, hours or quantity, rate and amount
  • Separate lines for premium hours, call pay and reimbursable expenses
  • Subtotal, any tax that applies to you, and the total due
  • How to pay: ACH details, check address or payment portal

Your Social Security number doesn't belong on an invoice; it goes on the W-9. For a ready-made layout, use our locum invoice template.

Payment terms and follow-up

  • Net 30 means payment is due 30 days after the invoice date. Hospitals and groups often have fixed payment runs, so ask which day invoices need to arrive.
  • Put the due date on the invoice as a date, so nobody has to count.
  • Follow up on a schedule: a short reminder a few days after the due date, then again a week later, by email to the billing contact.
  • Late fees only work if your contract includes them. Don't add one after the fact.

Records and taxes

Keep a copy of every invoice, your hours behind it and any receipts. The IRS lists invoices among the documents that support gross receipts (IRS recordkeeping). As a 1099 contractor, nobody withholds tax from your pay, so most people make quarterly estimated tax payments (IRS: Estimated taxes) and pay self-employment tax (IRS: Self-employment tax). A tax professional who works with independent clinicians can help you set this up.

In January, compare any 1099-NEC forms you receive against your own records. Clean invoices make that a five-minute job.
